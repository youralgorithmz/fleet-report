The Next Three Markets

Where to sign the next tree company, based on what makes Tree Masters of Savannah and our other strongest markets work. Three counties, three states, none of them worked by a current client.

The short version

Savannah is not lucky. It sits in a county where big old trees fall on houses far more often than anywhere else we work, homes are priced in the middle rather than the top, and fewer tree companies compete for the same phone. Our other best markets share most of that shape. We screened every county in the country for the same shape, threw out the ones that only look good on paper, and these three are left.

"Trees on property, per 10k" counts National Weather Service storm reports from 2015 to 2025 that describe a tree or limb landing on a house, roof, vehicle or power line, per 10,000 residents. Savannah's county scores 5.8. The typical county we work today scores 1.4.

Why Savannah

Savannah books nearly twice as many appointments per homeowner as a typical market

Raw appointment counts reward big lists. The fair comparison is how many appointments a market produces for every 10,000 homeowners on its list. In August, Savannah booked 14 per 10,000 and kept 9.5 after cancellations. The typical market booked 8 and kept 5.6. Only three other markets did better on kept appointments, and two of them are the ones Houston County, Alabama is modeled on.

The pattern

Four things our best markets have in common

We joined every market to public data on storms, home prices, population and the number of tree and landscaping companies. Across 29 markets, one thing predicted appointments far better than anything else: how often trees come down on property. Storm frequency by itself predicted almost nothing. Thunderstorms don't book appointments. Trees landing on roofs do.

One surprise worth saying out loud: cheaper markets booked better, not worse. Our most expensive markets are also our largest, most crowded metros, so this is partly a size effect. But it means the affluent suburb is not automatically the prize.

The rest of the screen

Thirteen counties matched Savannah's shape. Here is why these three.

The screen compared every county in the country to Savannah's on the measures that actually moved appointments. Six of the thirteen were in South Carolina or North Carolina, so the one-per-state rule alone forced choices. Then three exclusions did the rest.

  • Bay County, Florida scores second and should not be trusted. Hurricane Michael took out roughly 80% of its tree canopy in 2018. The storm history that lifts its score is the same event that removed the trees the work depends on. We measure where trees have fallen, not where trees still stand.
  • Bulloch County, Georgia is Savannah's own back yard. It is a good county, but it is an expansion for Tree Masters, not a new company in a new state.
  • The rest of the Carolinas are the bench. Berkeley County, SC borders Dorchester and is the natural second tier there. Johnston County, NC is the pick if we want a third Carolina account instead of Alabama.

Honest limits

What this analysis cannot tell you

  • One month of history. The calendar only returns appointments from 3 August. "Savannah performs week after week" is a four-week claim.
  • Twenty-nine markets. That is enough to see a pattern and not enough to prove one. Home value, market size and competitor density all move together, so we cannot fully separate them.
  • Where trees have fallen, not where they stand. There is no public county-by-county tree-canopy count, so storm damage reports stand in for it. That is exactly why Bay County was excluded.
  • Yield is per homeowner on the list, not per text delivered. Delivery data has never been joined to appointments.
  • Nobody has measured attendance. Every account shows zero showed and zero no-show, so "kept" means still on the calendar, not that the contractor met the homeowner.
  • The most recent week is not usable. The week ending 6 September was depressed across the whole fleet by a platform problem, so this brief uses August.
